The Quantum Gravity problem

The quantum gravity (QG) problem is the task of understanding gravity at all scales. These range from the size of the observable Universe (\(\sim 10^{26}\) m) down to the Planck length (\(\ell_P \sim 10^{-35}\) m) and possibly beyond. Since gravity couples universally to matter, and matter is quantum, the gravitational field is expected to show quantum (or at least fundamentally stochastic) behaviour at some scale. Otherwise the superposition principle would be violated. Rather than a single problem, QG is a set of related challenges, reviewed in [AsPe14, BaMoSh24].

Core tensions. Treated as a quantum field theory (QFT), general relativity (GR) is perturbatively non-renormalizable [HoVe74, GoSa86]. This can be traced to the dimensionality of Newton’s constant, \([G]=\text{mass}^{-2}\) in natural units. Adding higher-curvature terms restores renormalizability but spoils perturbative unitarity [Stel77]. At a more basic level, GR is written in the language of differential geometry, while quantum theory uses self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space. A framework is needed that accommodates both, defining a background-independent algebra of observables, its representation and its transition amplitudes without presupposing a classical manifold. The problem of time is a sharp instance of this tension. Time is dynamical in GR but an external parameter in quantum mechanics. In canonical quantization the Wheeler–DeWitt equation describes a “frozen” wavefunction of the Universe [DeWi67, Isha93], and one must explain how experienced time evolution emerges. Relational observables [Ditt06] and quantum reference frames [GiCaBr19] address this. Finally, diffeomorphism invariance makes physical observables intrinsically non-local [GiMaHa06]. QG may thus be seen as the search for a suitable representation, possibly deformed, of the diffeomorphism group and of the algebra of gravitational charges.

Effective field theory vs. UV completion. In the infrared (IR), perturbative QG is a well-defined effective field theory (EFT) [Dono94]. It systematically parametrizes deviations from Einstein gravity, possibly including additional geometric structures such as torsion and non-metricity [HMMN95] (see modified gravity). The central challenge is a UV completion that (i) yields finite, predictive observables, and (ii) reduces to GR at large distances, with the correct semiclassical limit. Since candidate completions rest on different principles and mathematical languages, they are increasingly compared at the level of the low-energy EFTs they produce [BBDK25, Bu24].

Broad strategies. Three broad routes exist (see also IR-UV mixing).

  • Fundamental reconstruction proposes new frameworks. Within QFT, it gives up perturbativity, as in asymptotic safety [Wein80, Reut98], or locality, as in non-local gravity [BGKM12, Mode12]. Beyond QFT there is string theory, with its extra dimensions, supergravity, dualities and M-theory [Polc98, Witt95]. There are also discrete and pre-geometric approaches such as loop quantum gravity, causal sets and causal dynamical triangulations [AsLe04, BLMS87, AmJuLo04]. Experimental signatures of these models are usually hard to extract.

  • Effective deformation modifies GR and quantum-mechanical symmetries to produce testable low-energy models. Examples are deformed algebras (the GUP, \(\kappa\)-Minkowski and noncommutative geometry) [KeMaMa95, MaRu94, DoFrRo95] and modified dispersion relations or Lorentz-symmetry violation and deformation. Here the difficulty is the converse: relating these effective models to fundamental principles.

  • Emergence takes spacetime and gravity as collective phenomena. This includes holography and AdS/CFT, which define QG non-perturbatively through a dual QFT [Hoof93, Suss95, Mald98]. It also includes condensed-matter-like emergence (gravity as thermodynamics) [Jaco95, Volo06], and “it from bit”, where geometry arises from entanglement and correlations [Whee89, Van 10]. See also emergent, nonclassical and fuzzy spacetime.

Emergence and the inverse problem. Whether gravity should be quantized or emerges from other degrees of freedom is itself open. By analogy, quantizing thermodynamics yields phonons, not statistical mechanics, whereas thermodynamics emerges by coarse-graining the microscopic degrees of freedom. The inverse problem is to recover classical, continuum geometry, time, causality and symmetries from an underlying structure through coarse-graining and the study of its phases. Some proposals push further, treating the Universe as a many-body quantum system, or tracing quantum behaviour itself to complex, chaotic gravitational interactions.

Black holes as theoretical probes. Black holes have a temperature and an entropy [Beke73], emit Hawking radiation [Hawk75], and raise the information paradox [Hawk76]. Recent semiclassical computations with quantum extremal surfaces, islands and replica wormholes reproduce the Page curve in controlled models [AHMS21]. Consistency criteria such as the weak gravity conjecture [AMNV07] further constrain candidate theories.

Phenomenology and observations. QG is increasingly constrained by data [Ad22]. Probes include early-universe cosmology and primordial fluctuations [HaHa83, AsSi11]. Black-hole environments are probed through quasinormal modes and ringdown [BeCaSt09] and shadows and photon rings [Ak19, Ak22]. High-energy astrophysics tests modified dispersion relations [AEMN98]. The growing interface with quantum information also motivates table-top experiments.

A QG theory is often hoped to also address singularity resolution, the cosmological constant problem [Wein89], the parameters and structure of the Standard Model, or dark matter and dark energy. These are welcome bonuses rather than requirements: the defining criteria remain UV consistency and the correct semiclassical limit.
